## One-sentence thesis
You can intend to act one way, and react another; two different traditions invite us to notice this gap.

## The necessary objection
Romans 7 addresses sin, law and the need for deliverance in Christ. The Fourth Way's many 'I's is a distinct model of inner life. Their similarity is a *question worth exploring*, not proof that Paul taught Gurdjieff's system or that the two are doctrinally equivalent.

## REEL / 45 seconds — original script

You decided who you would be this morning. Then someone interrupted you.

**4–15s / Recognition**
You planned to respond calmly. But a few words later, you heard yourself defending, attacking, explaining. Where did your earlier intention go?

**15–27s / Sources**
In Romans 7, Paul writes about the painful distance between willing the good and actually doing it. The Fourth Way examines a different phenomenon: shifting and competing inner “I”s.

**27–39s / Correct distinction**
They are not the same teaching. Paul's argument is theological. But reading these perspectives together gives us a surprisingly practical question.

**39–45s / Experiment**
When your intention disappears today, can you notice the exact moment? Don't answer from memory. Watch.

## CAPTION / draft
A promise made at breakfast can disappear by noon.

Romans 7 and the Fourth Way approach the experience of inner division from very different traditions. Placing them side by side doesn't make them the same teaching. It gives us a question to investigate: what happens in the moment intention gives way to reaction?

Try observing one ordinary interruption today—without inventing a story about yourself afterward.
